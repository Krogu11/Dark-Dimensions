# The Shattered Realms

> Current canon draft. Names and details may evolve as the campaign gains authored story content.

## The fracture

No surviving calendar agrees on when the world broke. Old monasteries call it the Sundering; caravan songs call it the Night Without Distance. Both describe the same impossible event: horizons folding across one another, roads reaching cities that had never shared a border, and creatures arriving from histories that were not their own.

The fractures became known as **Dark Dimensions**—places where the rules of one reality bleed into another. Most are invisible until approached. A quiet forest may become an ancient battlefield after sunset. A familiar tunnel may descend into a kingdom that vanished centuries ago. The deepest dungeons are not ruins beneath the earth at all, but wounds leading somewhere else.

Civilization did not disappear. It contracted behind walls.

## The old roads

The roads survived the fracture better than kingdoms did. No one knows who laid their oldest stones. They cross rivers where no bridge should stand, bend around mountains that moved during the Sundering, and sometimes lead travelers home by a route they have never walked.

For merchants and armies, roads are lifelines. For the Wanderer, they are a choice between speed and predictability: safer than the wilds, but watched by patrols, toll collectors, raiders, and creatures patient enough to learn where people must travel.

## The Wanderer

Every run begins with a different past but the same title. A Wanderer belongs to no throne and enters the realm without legal claim to land or army. Authority must be assembled one contract, one victory, and one surviving companion at a time.

Some Wanderers were raised behind city walls. Others learned the frontier, the forest, a cloister, a militia yard, or the underside of a market bridge. Their defining moment may have been an oath, exile, pilgrimage, or the simple fact that nobody else survived.

The title is older than any current faction. To common people it can mean savior, mercenary, grave robber, or approaching disaster.

## The three powers

### The Ember Crown

The Ember Crown claims continuity with the last royal dynasty before the Sundering. Its rulers preserve bloodlines, seals, ceremonies, and the fiction that every independent settlement is merely a province waiting to return.

Its roads are guarded, its taxes are legible, and its promises are written carefully. Supporters see the best chance to restore lawful civilization. Critics see a dying monarchy feeding every fresh flame into an ancient crown.

**Values:** oath, inheritance, stability, controlled trade.  
**Military character:** organized levies, cavalry traditions, royal vanguards.  
**Natural rival:** the Gloam Compact.

### The Gloam Compact

The Compact is not a kingdom. It is a web of councils, hidden patrons, smugglers, scholars, and border cities that survived by sharing information before sharing loyalty. Its agreements are often secret and always conditional.

The Compact studies dimensional fractures more openly than its rivals. This has produced useful knowledge, profitable contraband, and several disasters its agents insist never occurred.

**Values:** autonomy, information, leverage, adaptation.  
**Military character:** scouts, ambushers, specialists, chosen warbands.  
**Natural rivals:** both the Ember Crown and Iron Concord.

### The Iron Concord

The Iron Concord began as a mutual-defense pact between foundries, mining settlements, and fortress cities. It believes survival is an engineering problem: walls can be thickened, roads secured, stores counted, and soldiers trained until chaos has nowhere left to enter.

Its discipline protects thousands, but the Concord has little patience for weakness or local independence when either threatens collective security.

**Values:** duty, industry, discipline, preparedness.  
**Military character:** heavy infantry, fortified garrisons, professional armies.  
**Uneasy ally:** the Ember Crown.

## Peoples beyond the walls

### Humans

Humans dominate the remaining walled settlements because they inherited most of them, not because the world belongs to them. Their strength is adaptability: a frightened village levy can, with enough surviving battles, become a soldier, specialist, knight, ranger, or priest.

### Kobolds

Kobolds inhabit tunnel networks older than surface maps. They are scavengers, trappers, miners, and patient observers of larger peoples. Surface stories portray them as pests; their kings remember treaties that human dynasties have forgotten.

Defeating the Kobold King opens the possibility of beginning a future run as one of the tunnel-born.

### Orcs

The orcs endured the ash wastes by building strength into every custom. Outsiders see raiders and warlords. Orcs see clans forced to compete for land made barren by someone else's catastrophe.

Defeating an Orc Warlord proves enough strength to begin a future run among their kind.

### Revenants

A revenant is not simply an undead corpse. It is a person whose road continued after death should have ended it. Some retain memory and purpose; others possess only the certainty that something remains unfinished.

Reaching great personal experience allows a future Wanderer to begin as one of these returned souls.

## Warbands

A Warband is more than a deck of cards. Each card represents a persistent individual or troop identity with wounds, wages, experience, and a place beneath the Wanderer's banner. Only those actually committed to battle learn from it. Survivors accumulate experience and branch into more specialized forms of service.

Cities never have endless volunteers. A prosperous city with a strong garrison may occasionally produce a trained and expensive candidate; a poorer refuge may offer only a handful of levies. A commander who wants a diverse army must keep moving.

## Cities and scarcity

The walls protect people, not abundance. Every city depends on surrounding villages, roads, and caravans. Grain becomes flour, flour becomes bread, milk becomes cheese, clay becomes pottery, and each interrupted route creates an opportunity for hunger or profit.

Population determines how many hands a city can spare. Prosperity determines what those hands have learned. Garrison strength determines whether skilled soldiers remain available—or have already been called to the walls.

Names change from run to run because each generated realm is a different arrangement of surviving history. Ashford in one dimension may never have existed in the next.

## Death and another world

The Ironman rule is part of the game's fiction as well as its structure. When the Wanderer dies, that campaign is finished. Starting again does not rewind the same history; it enters another fractured arrangement of the realms, with different roads, settlements, dangers, and opportunities.

What persists between runs is not a corrected timeline, but knowledge—and eventually the right to begin from stranger origins.
